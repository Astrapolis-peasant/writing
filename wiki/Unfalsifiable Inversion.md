# Unfalsifiable Inversion

An argumentative structure that takes an existing thesis, reverses its polarity,
and is then built so that every possible observation — including all the evidence
for the original thesis — confirms the reversal.

Named here from [[The Manipulated Man (Vilar)]], but the shape is general and
recurs wherever a total explanation is offered for a social arrangement. The vault
now holds two specimens; a third would make this a pattern worth naming in
someone else's vocabulary rather than ours.

## The mechanism

The move has two steps. First, invert: whatever was read as evidence of A's power
over B becomes evidence of B's power over A. Second — and this is what makes it
airtight — supply a reason why the inversion is invisible to its own subjects, so
that testimony against the thesis becomes testimony for it.

In Vilar's case the invisibility mechanism is conditioning: men are trained from
infancy to experience servitude as pride, so their reports of feeling powerful are
exactly what the theory predicts. Women's reports of oppression are manipulation
via self-abasement. Every witness is disqualified in advance.

## The closure

| Observation | Reading under the thesis |
|---|---|
| Women do not work | They are parasitic on men |
| Women do work | Offices are "marriage markets"; the degree is a dowry |
| Women report oppression | Self-abasement, the core manipulation technique |
| Women report contentment | Confirms the parasitism |
| Women organize politically | "Emancipated" women exploit their husbands harder |
| Men report feeling powerful | The illusion doing its work |
| Men report feeling exploited | Rare; truth briefly surfacing |

No result is excluded. A theory that cannot lose has not won.

## A second specimen

[[The Satanic Bible (LaVey)]] performs the same operation on Christian morality
that Vilar performs on feminism, and states the method outright: "The Satanic
religion has not merely lifted the coin — it has flipped it completely over." Sin
becomes appetite, humility becomes cowardice, altruism becomes disguised
self-interest, and the beatitudes are reprinted with every term reversed.

Its theory of curses is a purer case of closure than anything in Vilar, because the
disconfirming evidence is explicitly converted into the strongest confirmation.
Believers are receptive to a curse; skeptics are *more* vulnerable, since denial
drives the fear into the unconscious where it "will multiply with each succeeding
misfortune." Subsequent misfortune counts as success, and the victim's insistence
that it was coincidence is precisely what the theory predicts. LaVey draws the
practical conclusion without embarrassment: "never attempt to convince the skeptic
upon whom you wish to place a curse. Allow him to scoff."

The two books are worth holding side by side. Published two years apart, both
invert a reigning moral framework wholesale, both organize the inversion around
strength against weakness, and both treat their opponents' testimony as evidence
for the thesis. LaVey's Book of Satan is lifted from Ragnar Redbeard's *Might Is
Right* ([[The Sources of the Satanic Bible]]); Vilar cites no such ancestor, so the
resemblance is structural rather than genealogical — *(inferred)*, and not a claim
of influence in either direction.

## Why it persuades anyway

The structure is not merely a defect — it explains the book's durability. A total
inversion is genuinely generative: it forces a reader to re-derive familiar facts
from an unfamiliar premise, and some of those re-derivations are sharp. Vilar's
claim that cosmetics are performed for other women rather than for men is a real
observation that her opponents had not anticipated, and it survives independently
of the framework that produced it.

The reading discipline this implies: separate the local observations, which can be
checked one at a time, from the totalizing frame, which cannot be checked at all.
Applied to Vilar, that separation yields [[Male Disposability]] on one side and an
unfalsifiable psychology on the other.
