# The Manipulated Man (Vilar)

A 1971 polemic by [[Esther Vilar]] arguing the exact inversion of the feminist
thesis: that men are not women's oppressors but their livestock, conditioned from
boyhood to experience their own exploitation as pride and to call it love.

The edition in `raw/` includes an author's introduction written roughly
thirty-five years later, in which Vilar calls the original "a pamphlet written in
great anger against the women's movement's worldwide monopoly of opinion."

## The argument

Men work; women do not, or only temporarily. Everything else follows from that
asymmetry. A man's intelligence, strength, and curiosity are not qualifications
for power but "qualifications for slavery" — the traits that make him worth
exploiting. He is trained into the arrangement by a system of conditioning
installed before he can evaluate it:

- **Praise-addiction.** Rewarded selectively from infancy, he learns to measure
  his worth by a woman's estimate of his usefulness.
- **Self-abasement.** She calls her own work worthless and his indispensable
  ("I'm so bad at things like that"), so he volunteers for everything she declines.
- **Sex as reward.** His libido is the one need he cannot self-supply, which sets
  the exchange rate for the whole relationship.
- **Children as hostages.** They justify his servitude after desire fades and bind
  him to one particular woman.

The result is a man who cannot conceive of freedom as anything but a threat —
"pleasure in non-freedom" — and a woman installed as his personal deity.

## The inversion

Vilar's method is to re-read every datum offered as evidence of female oppression
as evidence of female advantage. Housework is leisure, not labor. Male
professional dominance is a life sentence. Chivalry is tribute. Cosmetics are not
imposed by men but performed for other women, in a signalling system men cannot
even read — her sharpest and least anticipated claim ([[Unfalsifiable Inversion]]).

## Evidential status

> [!warning] Unsourced
> Across roughly 42,000 words the book offers three statistics, none attributed:
> that half of US private capital is held by women, that 13% of female American
> university students complete degrees, and that 51% of American men believe white
> women are as badly off as Black men. Every other claim is anecdote-as-archetype
> or flat assertion. Vilar's own framing — "a pamphlet" — is accurate. This page
> records what the book argues, not what is established.

The book separates cleanly into two layers. One is checkable and partly survives:
the disadvantages catalogued in the 2008 introduction — conscription, war deaths,
later retirement, no reproductive veto, one-way support obligations, custody loss
([[Male Disposability]]). The other is asserted flat and does not: that women lack
feelings, do not love their children, undergo intellectual atrophy from about age
five, and exaggerate the pain of childbirth. The second layer is what makes the
first unusable by association.

## Internal strains

Two tensions the book does not resolve:

- Women are described as mentally atrophied, yet operate a multigenerational
  manipulation system no man has ever detected. Vilar patches this — stupidity
  "makes them inaccessible to scrutiny" — but the patch does not carry the weight.
- Women both freely choose parasitism *and* are conditioned into it from age five
  by their mothers. She needs the agency to sustain the indictment and the
  conditioning to explain transmission.

## No remedy

The final chapter concedes that only women can break the cycle and that they will
not, "for there is absolutely no compelling reason why she should." The 2008
introduction asserts that measures to end it exist but names none. It is a
systemic-injustice argument with no prescription.
