# Vilar contra Second-Wave Feminism

[[Esther Vilar]]'s quarrel with the women's movement is not that it demanded too
much but that it accepted men's account of women — and so, on her reading, argued
women into the role of charity case while leaving their actual advantage intact.

This page states her argument. It does not adjudicate it, and her characterizations
of the authors below are hers, not summaries drawn from their own work.

## The charge against the theorists

Her central accusation is one of derivative thinking
([[The Manipulated Man (Vilar)]]). Of Simone de Beauvoir's *The Second Sex* (1949)
she writes that it
"could have been the first book on the subject of women" but instead became "a
handbook of Freud's, Marx's, Kant's, etc., ideas about women" — de Beauvoir
researched what men had written about women, found evidence of disadvantage
everywhere, and supplied it with a woman's signature. Betty Friedan, Kate Millett,
and Germaine Greer follow as "each a repetition of the last."

The inversion she thinks they missed: rather than being unmasked as "the most
cunning slave traders in history," women were "undersold" into the position of
victim. She reads the arrangement as flattering to men — the word *tyrant*
registers to a conditioned man as a compliment — which is why she thinks it met so
little resistance.

## Why she says the movement began in America

Not despite American women's advantages but because of them. Her account: the
system that benefits most American women turns hardest against the minority
unattractive by male standards, who cannot obtain a provider and must work. The
gap between the kept woman and the working woman is widest in the US, so the
revolt started where women were best off, among those excluded from the benefit
([[Unfalsifiable Inversion]] — note that this reasoning also makes the movement's
origin unable to count against her thesis).

## Why she says it failed

She claims the demands were granted almost immediately — night-shift work, heavy
trades, military service, alimony paid to men, a wife's separate legal personality
— and then went largely unused. "The army of suppressed women eagerly awaiting
that moment of liberation simply never materialized." Once a few women had been
photographed up a telephone pole, the uproar subsided, because, given a genuine
choice, most people decline drudgery and combat.

Her definition follows from this: "Liberation of women would mean her abdication
from the privileges she now has" — and a movement seeking additional rights while
retaining exemptions is, in her word, reactionary.

> [!warning] Unsourced
> Every factual claim above — what was legally granted, when, and how it was taken
> up — is asserted by Vilar without citation. The account of second-wave outcomes
> is contestable and is recorded here as her position, not as history.

## What the argument is worth

The evaluable residue is narrow. Her critique lands only against a strong claim no
serious opponent need hold — that women in 1971 were uniformly and involuntarily
subjugated. Against the weaker and more common claim, that women faced systematic
legal and economic constraints varying by class and country, it has no purchase,
and she explicitly brackets class differences as "the subject of many other books."

What survives is the narrower structural observation: that a movement framed
entirely as the removal of burdens has no vocabulary for the burdens it does not
seek to remove, and no account of the exemptions it keeps. That is a real gap, and
it is the same gap [[Male Disposability]] tries to name.
